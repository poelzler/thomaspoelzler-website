Whether morality is objective is one of the oldest questions in metaethics. A closely related question has received much less attention: do ordinary people take morality to be objective? This book examines the empirical research on folk moral objectivism from a philosophical perspective. Its first part argues that existing studies have measured lay people’s objectivist beliefs only to a limited extent. Still, their results suggest that most lay people are not objectivists. The second part asks what these findings imply for philosophical theories about the objectivity of morality, and suggests that the truth may lie beyond the traditional divide between objectivism and non-objectivism. The book is written for upper-level undergraduates, graduate students and researchers in moral psychology, metaethics, experimental philosophy and related fields.

Moral Reality and the Empirical Sciences

Series: Routledge Studies in Ethics and Moral Theory

Are there objective moral truths? This book asks what the empirical sciences, such as psychology, neuroscience, biology and anthropology, can contribute to answering this question. It defends two main claims. First, empirical findings can in principle bear on the debate between moral realists and anti-realists. Second, most of the science-based arguments that have been put forward so far lack sufficient empirical support. The book examines four such arguments in detail: the presumptive argument from folk moral realism, the argument from moral disagreement, the sentimentalist argument from the role of emotions in moral judgement, and the argument from evolutionary debunking. For each of them it reviews the available evidence and proposes how future research could test its empirical premises more rigorously.
